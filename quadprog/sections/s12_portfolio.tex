\section{A problem the synthetic families were not standing in for}
\label{sec:portfolio}

Every experiment so far draws its Hessian as $G = BB\T + nI$ for Gaussian $B$.
Section~\ref{ssec:limits} names that as a limitation and says the bias runs against
the method the paper advocates. This section makes the claim concrete rather than
leaving it as a caveat, by solving the problem the method was built for --- the
long-only portfolio --- on two real return series.

The program is~\eqref{eq:qp2} with the budget as the single equality and
non-negativity as the bounds,
\begin{equation}
  \min_{w}\; \tfrac12 w\T \Sigma w - \rho\,\mu\T w
  \quad\text{subject to}\quad
  \mathbf{1}\T w = 1, \quad w \ge 0,
  \label{eq:portfolio}
\end{equation}
with $\Sigma$ the annualised sample covariance of daily returns and $\mu$ the
sample mean. Two universes, so that nothing below is a property of one market:
the S\&P~500 at $n = \qpPfSpN{}$ assets over $T = \qpPfSpT{}$ days, and the
FTSE~100 at $n = \qpPfFtseN{}$ over $T = \qpPfFtseT{}$.

\begin{remark}[The data needs one filter, and the solver is right to insist]
The FTSE series carries an instrument quoted flat across the whole window. Its
sample variance is zero, which makes $\Sigma$ singular --- rank $86$ of $87$ ---
and \eqref{eq:portfolio} is then not strictly convex, so the factorisation of
Section~\ref{sec:factorisation} does not exist and the solver refuses the problem.
That refusal is correct and worth having: an asset with no variance has no place
in a variance-minimising portfolio, and a solver that silently regularised it away
would be hiding a modelling error. The degenerate column is dropped, and $n = 86$
below is the result.
\end{remark}

Three differences from the synthetic families, all of them in the same direction.

\paragraph{The conditioning is worse by orders of magnitude.} $\kappa(\Sigma)$ is
$\qpPfSpKappa$ on the S\&P~500 against the $O(n)$ of a Gaussian $BB\T + nI$. A
sample covariance of returns is close to low rank plus diagonal, which is exactly
the structure that makes it ill-conditioned, and it is the structure real data
has.

\paragraph{The active set is nearly everything.} At the minimum-variance corner
the solver holds $\qpPfSpActive{}$ of $\qpPfSpN{} + 1$ constraints active on the
S\&P~500, and the optimal portfolio holds only $\qpPfSpNames{}$ names. That is not
a quirk: minimum variance concentrates, and long-only minimum variance concentrates
onto a vertex at which almost every bound binds. A well-conditioned Hessian spreads
the optimum instead, which is why the synthetic families produce small active sets.

\paragraph{And so the exact walk is far longer.} It takes $\qpPfSpOuter{}$ outer
iterations here against the $\qpPdasBudgetOuter{}$ that the synthetic
budget-plus-bounds family produced at $n = 100$ in Section~\ref{ssec:pdas}. The
iteration count grows with the size of the set the walk must reach, and on real
data that set is large. Every statement in Section~\ref{ssec:pdas} about the
advantage of guessing the active set was therefore measured at the wrong end of
its range: on the S\&P~500 the fast path settles in $\qpPfSpRepairs{}$ repairs
against $\qpPfSpOuter{}$ outer iterations, and is $\qpPfFastSpeed\times$ faster
than the exact walk rather than the two-to-three of the synthetic case.

The comparison against the other solvers moves the same way. On the S\&P~500 the
exact walk is faster than the reference C implementation of the same algorithm ---
this is the large-$n$ side of the crossover of Section~\ref{ssec:compare} --- while
on the FTSE~100, at $n = \qpPfFtseN{}$, the reference is faster, which is the
small-$n$ side. Two points on one curve, on real data, without either being
constructed for the purpose.

\paragraph{Accuracy survives the conditioning.} The active-set method returns
$\qpPfSpResid$ on the S\&P~500 despite $\kappa(\Sigma) = \qpPfSpKappa$, while the
two iterative solvers return residuals seven to nine orders of magnitude larger at
the same requested tolerance. The reason is the one given in
Section~\ref{ssec:compare} and it is sharper here: the optimum is a vertex with
$\qpPfSpActive{}$ active constraints, which is the worst case for a method that
approaches the boundary asymptotically and the natural case for one that
terminates on it.

\subsection{The frontier is what warm starting is for}

A frontier sweep varies $\rho$ in~\eqref{eq:portfolio} and changes nothing else.
That is precisely the family of Section~\ref{sec:sweep}: $\Sigma$, $C$ and $b$
fixed, only the linear term moving. Figure~\ref{fig:portfolio} traces
$\qpPfPoints{}$ points across the S\&P~500 frontier.

\begin{figure}[ht]

\caption{Left: the long-only efficient frontier. Right: the number of names held

$\qpPfNamesLo{}$ at the return-seeking end, where the budget is concentrated in a

\label{fig:portfolio}
\end{figure}

Reusing the factorisation costs $\qpPfWarm$\,ms per point against
$\qpPfCold$\,ms solving each point from scratch, a factor of
$\qpPfSweepSpeed$. The cached active set stayed optimal on $\qpPfHits{}$ of the
$\qpPfPoints{}$ points, with $\qpPfMisses{}$ requiring the repair of
Proposition~\ref{prop:repair}; and the answers are identical to the cold solves,
because a reused point is returned only against the certificate of
Section~\ref{ssec:sweepcost}.

The right-hand panel is the reason the hit rate is as high as it is, and it is a
fact about the problem rather than about the solver. The number of names held
falls monotonically along the frontier, so consecutive points differ by a handful
of constraints out of nearly five hundred, and a cached set is usually still
optimal one step later. That is the structural property Section~\ref{sec:sweep}
assumes, appearing in the wild.

\begin{remark}[What this section does and does not claim]
It claims that the method's advantages are larger on the problems it was designed
for than on the synthetic families used to test it, and that the direction of that
gap was predicted rather than discovered after the fact. It does not claim that
this is the best way to build a portfolio. Minimum variance on a raw sample
covariance is a poor estimator, and the companion work in this
series~\cite{schmelzer2026nncg,schmelzer2026rmt} is about the estimation problem;
here $\Sigma$ is a source of realistically shaped matrices, and nothing in this
paper depends on how it was estimated.
\end{remark}
